When the exam is fixed and the weeks are counted, lesson quality is mostly a pacing question. Strong exam tutoring means knowing exactly where you are against the syllabus and the calendar, spending time where marks actually are, and keeping the student's workload sustainable through a long year. This guide sets out how to pace IB, DSE, and A-Level preparation without burning the student out.

## Reverse-plan from the exam date

Start by mapping the syllabus against the exam calendar. Work backwards from the exam date and block out the non-negotiables first: school mock exams, school assessment weeks, and public holidays. What remains is your actual teaching runway, and it is always shorter than parents assume.

Give each remaining week a rough theme rather than a detailed plan for every lesson. Term-level themes survive contact with reality; a week-by-week timetable drawn in September usually does not. If you have not run a diagnostic yet, [the first lesson](/help/pedagogy-excellence/first-trial-lesson-diagnostic) is where the plan's priorities should come from.

## Prioritise high-mark-weighted topics and weak spots

Not all syllabus units are equal. Before you allocate lesson time:

- **Weigh the marks.** Use the syllabus and past papers to identify which topics carry the most marks, and spend your hours there first.
- **Overlay the student's weak spots.** A high-mark topic the student already handles needs less attention than a mid-weight topic they cannot start.
- **Say the trade-offs out loud.** If there is not enough time to cover everything, tell the parent what you are dropping and why. Honest prioritisation beats a silent plan that quietly runs out of weeks.

## Alternate content coverage with timed practice

Content coverage and exam performance are different skills. A common failure mode is covering the whole syllabus beautifully and discovering in March that the student cannot finish a paper. Alternate deliberately: some lessons build content, others are timed past-paper practice under realistic conditions. As the exam approaches, tilt the ratio towards timed papers, and review the mark scheme together afterwards so the student learns how examiners award marks.

## Teach exam technique explicitly

Do not assume technique transfers on its own. Teach it as content:

- **Command words.** What "explain", "discuss", "calculate", and "compare" each demand in this subject's mark scheme.
- **Mark-scheme language.** The phrasing examiners actually reward, and the acceptable shortcuts.
- **Time per mark.** A simple rule of thumb, for example roughly a minute per mark, and when to abandon a question and move on.

## Build in spaced review

A topic taught in October and never revisited is effectively untaught by May. Build spaced review into the plan so earlier topics resurface: a short retrieval quiz at the start of lessons, one past-paper question on an old topic each week, and a rotation that cycles back through the syllabus every few weeks. The [lesson blueprints](/help/pedagogy-excellence/lesson-blueprints) give structures you can slot review into.

## Watch for burnout and adjust the plan

Your students are also carrying school homework, internal assessments, and their own lives. When school workload spikes, adjust the plan rather than pushing through: lighten homework, shift a lesson towards the school assessment they are panicking about, or temporarily reduce the volume of timed practice. Keep communicating honestly with parents about realistic priorities; how to hold that conversation well is covered in [client mastery](/help/parent-communication/client-mastery) and [post-lesson feedback](/help/parent-communication/post-lesson-feedback).

## Use mock results to re-prioritise, never to panic

Mocks are the best diagnostic data you will ever get, and the worst emotional trigger in the tutoring relationship. When results come back:

- Re-run your prioritisation against the actual paper: which topics lost the most marks?
- Update the remaining weeks' themes accordingly.
- Frame the result to the student and parent as information, not verdict. Point to the specific fixes and the weeks remaining, not to the grade.

A calm, plan-led response to a disappointing mock is one of the strongest signals a parent can get that their tutor is in control of the process.
