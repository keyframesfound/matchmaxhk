Parents form their judgement of a tutor in the first lesson, and the worst thing you can do in that hour is talk. Treat Lesson 1 as a rapport-and-diagnostic session: your goal is to understand how the student thinks, find the real bottleneck, and leave with a study plan the parent has agreed to. This guide gives you a structure you can adapt to any subject.

## Run a low-stakes diagnostic

Open with a short quiz or a handful of past-paper questions on topics the student has recently covered at school. The point is not to grade them; it is to watch how they think. Pick questions that are recent enough to be familiar but varied enough to expose different failure modes.

While the student works, observe rather than intervene. Resist rushing to correct every step: a student who writes a wrong sign in line two but recovers by line five tells you something very different from one who cannot start at all. Where they hesitate, what they skip, and which questions they abandon are more informative than the final score.

## Identify the specific bottleneck

Almost every struggling student has one of three bottlenecks, and each needs a different plan:

- **Foundational gaps.** The current topic fails because an earlier one was never solid. Marking scheme corrections will not fix this; backfilling will.
- **Exam technique.** They know the content but lose marks to timing, misread command words, or incomplete answers.
- **Confidence.** They can do the work but freeze, guess early, or apologise for correct answers.

Name the bottleneck specifically. "Weak at maths" is not a diagnosis; "cannot set up linear equations from word problems" is something you can actually teach.

## Translate findings into a study plan

Turn the diagnostic into a short plan: what you will focus on, in what order, and how you will measure progress. Keep it to a page. The details of lesson structure can follow the [lesson blueprints](/help/pedagogy-excellence/lesson-blueprints), but the plan itself is what the parent is really buying.

## Agree the plan with the parent: the Rule of 3

Present your findings to the parent in three beats:

1. **What you observed.** One or two concrete examples from the diagnostic, not generalities.
2. **Why it matters.** How that bottleneck is costing marks or confidence right now.
3. **The next measurable step.** What you will do about it and what improvement will look like by a specific point, for example "accurate equation setup on 8 of 10 practice questions within four weeks".

This framing shows the parent you observed their child rather than performed at them. It also sets honest expectations: you are describing a process, not promising an outcome, because MatchMax does not guarantee grades or results.

## Propose a regular weekly slot, without pressure

At the end of the lesson, offer a regular weekly slot that fits your schedule and theirs. One clear proposal ("Would Tuesday 5pm work each week?") is more useful to a parent than "let me know whenever you're free". If they need time to decide, that is fine; say you will hold the slot for a few days. The mechanics of confirming times and handling changes are covered in [coordinating lesson dates](/help/scheduling-logistics/coordinate-lesson-dates), and if you have not yet made contact, start with [introducing yourself on WhatsApp](/help/parent-communication/introduce-yourself-whatsapp).

## A 60-minute structure you can adapt

| Minutes | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| 0 to 5 | Welcome, set expectations, start rapport |
| 5 to 20 | Diagnostic: low-stakes quiz or past-paper questions |
| 20 to 40 | Teach one targeted concept drawn from what you observed |
| 40 to 55 | Guided practice on that concept with the student leading |
| Last 5 | Set homework, confirm the next lesson time |

The structure is a frame, not a script. If the diagnostic reveals something urgent, let the teaching section run long and trim the practice.
